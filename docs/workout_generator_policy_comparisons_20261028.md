## What the diversity trial establishes

Moving family distribution ahead of source balance alone leaves Nordic exposure unchanged. Moving it to first place reduces the family score from 64 to 35 and introduces three barbell Romanian-deadlift sets without relaxing any hard volume requirement. The first four stages (family, known demand, duration and source balance) were optimal. Workload and later placement stages were only feasible.

This stronger diversity preference has material countervailing changes. Known unsupported-trunk exposure increases from 51 to 66 working sets weekly, with each session increasing from 17 to 22. That is an exposure count, not a fatigue, injury or personal-tolerance score. Axial-loading and eccentric-emphasis evidence remain incomplete and are not optimized as fully known groups.

Qualified horizontal push/pull exposure changes from 15/12 to 8/6; role-tagged rear-delt exposure changes from 12 to 2. The movement-balance score improves from 9 to 2 partly through reducing those quantities. All approved coverage and muscle-credit constraints still pass. These facts neither prove inadequate stimulus nor establish preserved development of every subregion. The present family objective minimizes absolute peaks and explicitly protects role exposure only through approved hard floors; it does not independently establish adequate exposure.

The diversity-first candidate estimates 78.3 / 75.0 / 79.2 minutes, with a 255-second spread. The native baseline estimates 81.8 / 80.6 / 79.7 minutes, with a 124-second spread. Both receive duration score zero because the approved soft tolerance treats spreads up to 300 seconds as equally balanced. No actual execution time was measured.

The RDL is standalone, not paired with Nordic curls. Its retained configuration has code-reported load headroom; personal execution capability remains unknown. These candidate facts do not authorize a claim that the plan is personally suitable.

## Interpretation and decision

Promoting meaningful diversity is a more direct intervention than relaxing a volume requirement solely to lower one exercise's count. The experiments establish that it can introduce an alternative hamstring family while preserving the original volume bands. They do not justify maximizing the present diversity score unconditionally ahead of every other preference.

Increasing the glute ceiling progressively trades Nordic work for more hip-thrust work and higher glute credits. Lowering the hamstring minimum reduces the required hamstring dose; it does not by itself introduce another hamstring family. None is an unqualified improvement in every relevant dimension.

Do not automatically apply any of these policies as a new default. The useful design direction is to favor family variety without confusing lower absolute role counts with better development, and to present the verified exposure tradeoffs explicitly. Which role exposure must be protected requires an approved programming decision; the retained results do not establish universal set quotas or an acceptable physiological demand threshold. The current evidence supports a choice among disclosed tradeoffs, not a new universal score or an exercise-specific cap.
